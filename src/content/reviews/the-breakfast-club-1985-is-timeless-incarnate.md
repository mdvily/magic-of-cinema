# What are some 80’s movies that could have been made today, for all we know? Star Wars Episode V: The Emperor Strikes Back (1980), E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (1982), Back To The Future (1985), Ferris Bueller’s Day Off (1986), and our focal movie, **The Breakfast Club (1985)**. The film is a definition of timeless, and my Number One film everyone needs to see at least once during their childhood. But how did this movie come to be? Well, it was written and directed by John Hughes, and the film was shot in a single location: Maine North High School in Des Plaines, Illinois. The actual library was too small, they built a larger library that was set inside the school gym. Hughes finished the script in a single weekend of 1982, and originally named this, “The Lunch Bunch”. However, his friend had a son who used the term “Breakfast Club”, and the title was changed. Then, Universal Studios produced the film on a budget of only $1 Million. But how is this definition of a 1980’s movie considered timeless? **SPOILERS!**

# **Story**

# The premise of the film is imaginative, in a way where many directors would not make or get made a film about five teenagers in a detention for nine hours, and time passes in the room as the kids open up to each other. That is exactly what The Breakfast Club (1985) is about. Some people’s biggest criticism is that the film is very mean spirited. I find that true at the start, but it works for two reasons. One: the characters become kinder towards each other as the movie progresses. Two: This movie had to be mean spirited so it would fit with teenagers, especially teens at the time period, and these are kids who did bad things, one of which is a criminal. What did you expect? Now, onto the many positives. One: there is a great reason why the film goes nowhere. It does go to places, but you never know because it stays in one room. This makes for some very clever character deconstructions: John breaks the law because he has abusive parents. Brian is a goody-two-shoes, because his mother is very hard on him. Andrew acted up, because his father pressured him into doing so. Claire is spoiled because she feels invisible, like an argument device for her parents. And Allison hides her true self because her parents seem to want nothing to do with their daughter. While these traits perfectly explain why the characters do what they do, they do not excuse the characters’ flaws. This forces them to grow as people. And the character development would not be as good as it is, if I disliked the pacing. That is not the case. The film is paced so slowly and naturally, which makes the characters feel developed and the story good at making sense. Also, the comedy is hilarious. The jokes fit perfectly with what the characters are feeling and the actors give in to these moments. However, there is a lot of heart in the picture. A key example is the characters dancing together, or Andrew kissing Allison, and John with Claire after she gives him her earring. **VERDICT: 20/20**

# **Visuals**

# The brain, athlete, basket case, princess, and criminal’s movie has quite amazing visuals, even if the script gets more love. First off: the clothing and color. The clothing color the characters wear perfectly affects what I saw them as: For Brian, he just wears a plain green shirt, and he gets treated as a loser. Allison wears black, and gets treated as mysterious, but as her blue eyelids disappear, we finish learning everything about her. Claire wears pink not to show how rich she is, but how spoiled she is. John wears a red polo shirt, with black gloves and a blue denim jean jacket. This shows him as a criminal with a good heart. Andrew has a blue onesie, opposite to John’s shirt, showing how he despises him at the start. I actually am glad that they keep these outfits in the end, because not doing so would be weird. The main color is yellow, which gives you this teenager-ish, lived in feel. The cinematography is great, as it is close enough to the characters for you to know their thoughts, but not close enough for it to be uncomfortable. My favorite part of the cinematography though, is when the camera rotates. It becomes ideal for us in terms of knowing what the characters are thinking. **VERDICT: 20/20**

# **Characters**

# So, everything else is magnificent. But what about the characters? Well, they might be the greatest aspect of this movie. Now, there are six main characters, and I feel like giving a mention to each member of this breakfast club. John Bender (The criminal who pulled a false fire alarm) is their leader and the definition of a flawed hero. Yes, John makes mistakes and does awful deeds, such as flirting inappropriately with Claire, smoking underage, and disrespecting Vernon on purpose. But you have to remember how young he is. However, as the film progresses, John also shows his good deeds, such as taking responsibility for his mistakes, telling the truth about how his home life is not the best, and standing up for his peers. His character arc is realizing that he does not let his parents’ abuse determine who he is as a person. Then you have Brian Johnson (The brain who brought a flare to school, so he could commit suicide for failing a school assignment). He starts off believing his worth in life is determined by his intelligence, and only his intelligence. He is most of the humorous comic relief, but he actually has a purpose in the story, and character growth. Andrew Clark (The athlete who taped another student’s butt together) is the opposite of John. While John makes Claire uncomfortable, Andrew stands up for her, and is more casual to Allison. His flaw? He would rather bully someone for his father’s approval than have empathy. Claire Standish (The princess who skipped school to shop at the mall) is up next. Claire has the strongest character arc of the five, as she learns how to not let peer pressure get to her. She starts seeing John as a friend, not a freak and goes from telling Allison to shut up, to giving her a soft makeover. A great example of attention to detail at its best. Lastly, we have Allison Reynolds (The basket case who came to detention because she simply had nothing better to do). Allison has the character arc of not letting her parents’ neglect make her hide herself from everyone. However, a wonderful character growth moment for her is her decision to let Claire talk to her about what happens at her home. Assistant Principal Richard “Dick” Vernon is the antagonist. Now, while trying to keep misbehaving students in line is a good thing, it is about how he does it. Verbal abuse, even telling John that when he never amounts to anything in life, he will be there. But his attribute is that him assigning the essay actually assembles the five. **VERDICT: 20/20**

# **Acting**

# If only one movie of the 80’s could have perfect acting, this would be it. Judd Nelson is John Bender. Nelson gives you a performance that is unsettling, and humorous at the same time. The unsettling part is him inappropriately flirting with Ringwald (Fun fact: he stayed in character, doing that outside of filming and before Gleason defended him, he almost got fired). The humorous part is his bad boy persona. Anthony Michael Hall is Brian Johnson. Michael Hall starts off as overtly serious, but in the end, you can see him starting to take his life out of school with a smile. We see sad faces on him at the start and happy ones at the end. Emilio Estevez is Andrew Clark. He really has that urge to defend Claire at first, and in the end, shows softer body movements to Judd. Molly Ringwald was Claire Standish. Ringwald always had a soft voice. The change is in her body movements. She starts off as snappy in movements, and ends up as softer. Ally Sheedy is Allison Reynolds. Sheedy fully turns into a “weird kid”, and slowly gets a bigger smile on her face. What I love about the five actors is that as their characters change, so do they. The late Paul Gleason is Assistant Principal Vernon. Gleason feels like half the time a cynical rule stick-up, and the other half a bully to Nelson. Both of those are true about his character. **VERDICT: 20/20**

# **Music**

# The music is incredible. It always knows how to present itself for every single scene. For the original tracks, you have "Dream Montage" and "Love Theme". They help ground the film in the emotional department, and make it feel soft. This perfectly makes you care for the emotional scenes and make them feel real. "Fire in the Twilight" is the song that plays as the teenagers try escaping without Assistant Principal Vernon catching them. That is a good pun, as the characters are burning in the need to get out of the place. "We Are Not Alone" is the dancing theme, that works here, because it is about getting together. And what do the characters do? They become friends. But my favorite song is "Don't You (Forget About Me)" by Simple Minds. The song kind of leaves the ending ambiguous, because the film does not explain if the characters forget about each other after they leave detention. **VERDICT: 20/20**

# **In conclusion**, this movie is a rare bird. The type of bird that can perfectly fly. The Breakfast Club (1985) is a flawless feature that leaves you a changed person. I really thought first watching this with my parents and mother’s parents last June was something I was too young for. I was dead wrong. It should therefore come as so surprise that I would recommend this film to any teenager above the age of 13. **Final Score: 100%**
